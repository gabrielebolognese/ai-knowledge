A **convolutional neural network** (CNN) is a neural network whose main layers slide a small grid of learned <Term slug="weight">weights</Term> across an image instead of connecting every pixel to every unit.<Cite id="cs231n-cnn" /> Because the same small grid is reused at every position, a pattern learned in one corner is recognised anywhere, and the network needs far fewer parameters than a fully connected one.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-cnn" /> CNNs read the digits, letters and doodles in this course's three vision projects.

## Why a plain MLP struggles with images

A <Term slug="multilayer-perceptron" /> flattens a 28×28 image into 784 unrelated numbers. It has no idea that neighbouring pixels belong together, and a "7" moved two pixels to the right lands on completely different inputs, so it must be learned again.<Cite id="d2l-cnn" /> A fully connected layer from 784 pixels to just 128 units already needs 100,480 weights and biases, and every one of them is tied to a single pixel position.

Images have two properties a better layer can exploit. Useful patterns, such as edges and stroke ends, are *local*: they occupy a few neighbouring pixels. And the same pattern can appear *anywhere*. A convolutional layer builds both facts into its structure.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-cnn" />

## The convolution, by hand

A <Term slug="convolution" /> layer holds a small grid of weights, the **kernel** or filter, typically 3×3. It lays the kernel on a patch of the image, multiplies the overlapping numbers pairwise, adds them up together with a <Term slug="bias" />, and writes the result into an output grid. Then it slides one step and repeats. The output grid is called a <Term slug="feature-map" />.<Cite id="cs231n-cnn" />

<Analogy breaks="Real kernels are learned from data, not designed, and many of them look nothing like a neat stencil.">
Think of a small torch with a stencil cut into it, swept over a picture row by row. At each spot a meter reads how well what it sees matches the stencil; the sheet of meter readings is the feature map.
</Analogy>

Strictly, this operation is *cross-correlation*: textbook convolution flips the kernel first. Deep learning libraries skip the flip, and since the kernel is learned the difference does not matter.<Cite id="d2l-cnn" /><Cite id="pt-conv2d" />

Two settings control the output size. **Padding** adds a border of zeros so the kernel can also be centred on edge pixels, and **stride** is how many pixels the kernel jumps between positions.<Cite id="dumoulin2016" /> Dumoulin and Visin's guide works out the general rule:<Cite id="dumoulin2016" />

| Layer | $W, K, P, S$ | Output |
|---|---|---|

The LeNet-5 row matches the 28×28 maps in LeCun and colleagues' 1998 paper.<Cite id="lecun1998" /> The AlexNet paper states a 224-pixel input, which does not give a whole number; the CS231n notes point out that 227 is what makes the arithmetic work.<Cite id="cs231n-cnn" /> PyTorch's `Conv2d` documentation gives the same formula with an extra term for dilation.<Cite id="pt-conv2d" />

## Channels, weight sharing and parameters

A colour image has three input channels, and a hidden layer may have 32 or 64. A kernel always spans **all** input channels, and a layer with 64 kernels produces 64 output channels, one feature map each.<Cite id="cs231n-cnn" /> Because each kernel is reused at every position, the parameter count depends on the kernel and channel sizes but not on the image size. This **weight sharing** also makes the layer *equivariant* to shifts: move the input and the feature map moves with it.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-cnn" />

## Pooling and receptive fields

A <Term slug="max-pooling" /> layer keeps the largest value in each 2×2 block and, with stride 2, halves the width and height.<Cite id="pt-maxpool" /> It makes later layers cheaper and gives a little tolerance to small shifts, at the cost of exact position.<Cite id="cs231n-cnn" />

Each output value depends on a patch of the original image called its <Term slug="receptive-field" />. One 3×3 layer sees 3×3 pixels; two stacked see 5×5; three see 7×7. Simonyan and Zisserman argued that stacked 3×3 layers beat one large kernel: two 3×3 layers cost $18C^2$ weights against $25C^2$ for one 5×5, and add an extra non-linearity.<Cite id="simonyan2014" /> Pooling grows the receptive field faster still.

Stacking convolution, <Term slug="relu" /> and pooling builds a hierarchy. Zeiler and Fergus visualised trained networks and found early layers responding to edges and colours, and deeper layers to textures, object parts and whole objects.<Cite id="zeiler2014" /> At the end, the feature maps are flattened and passed to fully connected layers that output <Term slug="logits" />, trained with <Term slug="cross-entropy" /> as before.

<Callout type="pitfall">
CNNs tolerate shifts, not rotations. An upside-down 6 looks like a 9 to them, and EMNIST's letters, which are stored transposed, must be turned upright before training.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A convolution slides one small kernel over the image: multiply, add, add the bias, move.
- Output width is $\lfloor (W - K + 2P)/S \rfloor + 1$; "same" padding for a 3×3 kernel is $P = 1$.
- Weight sharing makes the parameter count independent of image size: $(K^2 C_{in} + 1) C_{out}$ per layer.
- Pooling and stacking grow the receptive field, building edges into strokes into shapes.
- The digits, letters and doodle projects all use the same 421,642-parameter CNN.

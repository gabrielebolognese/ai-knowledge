A **language model** assigns a probability to every possible next token given the tokens before it, written $P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$.<Cite id="d2l-lm" /> Generating text means asking for that distribution, picking one token, appending it and asking again. Training decides how good the probabilities are; the picking rule, called *decoding* or *sampling*, decides how the generated text reads, and it changes the output as much as many training choices do.<Cite id="hf-generate" />

## Next-token prediction

By the chain rule of probability, the probability of a whole sequence is the product of next-token probabilities: $P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_t P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$.<Cite id="d2l-lm" /> A model that predicts the next token well therefore models whole texts well. The idea is old: Claude Shannon measured in 1951 how well people could guess the next letter of English text, as a way to estimate its information content.<Cite id="shannon1951" />

Training uses a neat alignment trick. Take a window of $T+1$ consecutive tokens. The input is the first $T$ of them and the target is the same window shifted one position to the left, so position $t$ of the target holds the token that follows position $t$ of the input.<Cite id="d2l-lm" /> One window therefore gives $T$ training examples at once: predict token 2 from token 1, token 3 from tokens 1–2, and so on. With a <Term slug="causal-mask" /> or a recurrent network, each position sees only the tokens to its left, so no example can peek at its own answer.

## Loss and perplexity

The training loss is the <Term slug="cross-entropy" /> of the true next token, averaged over all positions: the mean of $-\ln p$ for the token that actually came next. Natural logarithms give the loss in *nats* per token.<Cite id="d2l-lm" /> **Perplexity** is the exponential of that average loss, and it has a friendly reading: the model is as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among that many tokens.<Cite id="wiki-perplexity" />

<Analogy breaks="A real model is not uniform over a fixed set of options; it is confident about some tokens and unsure about others, and perplexity is only their geometric average.">
Perplexity 8.5 means the model behaves, on average, like someone rolling a fair 8.5-sided die among the plausible next letters. A perfect model would have perplexity 1.
</Analogy>

Perplexity is only comparable between models that use the same tokenizer and the same test text, because a token's difficulty depends on how much text it covers.<Cite id="wiki-perplexity" />

## Decoding: from probabilities to text

The model's last layer outputs one raw score, a <Term slug="logits">logit</Term>, per vocabulary entry, and a <Term slug="softmax" /> turns the logits into probabilities. There are two extreme ways to pick the next token.<Cite id="hf-generate" /> <Term slug="greedy-decoding">Greedy decoding</Term> always takes the most probable token; it is deterministic and tends to repeat itself. *Pure sampling* draws a token at random in proportion to its probability; it is varied but now and then picks an unlikely token that derails the text. In PyTorch the draw is [`torch.multinomial`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.multinomial.html), which picks indices according to given weights.<Cite id="pt-multinomial" />

Three controls sit between those extremes. **Temperature** divides the logits by a number $T$ before the softmax; see <Term slug="sampling-temperature" />. **Top-k** keeps only the $k$ most likely tokens; Fan, Lewis and Dauphin used it with $k = 10$ for story generation.<Cite id="fan2018" /> **Top-p**, or <Term slug="nucleus-sampling" />, keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to at least $p$.<Cite id="holtzman2019" />

Temperature is how adventurous a regular diner is: at a low temperature they always order their favourite dish, at a high one they nearly pick from the menu at random. Top-k is "only consider the dishes on the specials board".
</Analogy>

## Why greedy decoding repeats itself

It seems natural that always picking the most likely token would give the best text. Holtzman and colleagues showed that it does not: maximisation-based decoding, greedy or beam search, produces text that is bland and strangely repetitive.<Cite id="holtzman2019" /> They found a feedback loop: each time a phrase repeats, the model assigns a higher probability to repeating it again, so a decoder that follows the maximum gets stuck.<Cite id="holtzman2019" /> Human-written text, by contrast, regularly uses tokens that the model did not rank first.

Pure sampling avoids the loop but has the opposite problem. The tail of the distribution, thousands of tokens with tiny probabilities each, adds up to a noticeable chance of drawing nonsense.<Cite id="holtzman2019" /> Nucleus sampling cuts that unreliable tail while keeping a candidate set that adapts to the model's confidence: a single token when the next word is obvious, many when it is open.<Cite id="holtzman2019" />

## Choosing settings

The right settings depend on the task, and the published values are starting points to tune by reading samples, not rules.<Cite id="hf-generate" /> For code completion, where a likely continuation is what the user wants, a low temperature with top-k works well; the course's autocompleter defaults to $T = 0.4$ and $k = 20$. For names and Shakespeare, a temperature near 0.8–1.0 keeps variety. The small-text project writes the rules once and every generator calls them:

## Summary

- A language model predicts $P(x_t \mid x_{<t})$; one window of $T+1$ tokens gives $T$ training examples through shifted targets.
- Loss is the average $-\ln p$ of the true next token; perplexity $= e^{L}$ reads as "choosing among this many equally likely tokens".
- Temperature rescales logits; top-k keeps a fixed number of candidates; top-p keeps a set that grows with uncertainty.
- Greedy decoding loops because repetition feeds on itself; truncated sampling breaks the loop without drawing from the junk tail.

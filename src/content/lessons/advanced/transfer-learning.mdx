**Transfer learning** reuses a network trained on one task as the starting point for another, instead of starting from random weights.<Cite id="wiki-transfer" /> Its most common form in deep learning is **fine-tuning**: take a model pretrained on a large dataset such as <Term slug="imagenet" />, replace its output layer, and train on your own, often much smaller, dataset.<Cite id="d2l-fine-tuning" /> It is the standard way to get good results with a few hundred labelled images.

## Why features transfer

A CNN trained on photographs learns a hierarchy: edges and colour blobs in the first layer, textures and parts in the middle, task-specific detectors at the end.<Cite id="yosinski2014" /> Yosinski, Clune, Bengio and Lipson measured this directly. They found that first-layer features look much the same whatever dataset the network was trained on, and that features become more specific to the original task layer by layer.<Cite id="yosinski2014" />

Their experiments also showed two costs of copying layers: higher layers are specialised to the original task, and splitting the network in the middle breaks units that had learned to work together. Transferability fell as the two tasks became less similar, yet even features from a distant task beat random weights. Most usefully, starting from transferred layers and then fine-tuning them gave *better* accuracy than training from scratch, even with plenty of target data.<Cite id="yosinski2014" />

<Analogy breaks="A driver knows which skills carry over; a network has no such insight, and the useful layers have to be found by experiment.">
Someone who can drive a car learns to drive a van in an afternoon: steering, traffic rules and judging distances carry over, and only the size and the mirrors are new.
</Analogy>

## The recipe: freeze, replace, train

The [torchvision models page](https://docs.pytorch.org/vision/stable/models.html) lists pretrained networks together with their weights and the exact preprocessing they expect.<Cite id="tv-models" /> For ResNet-18, `ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT` loads weights that reach 69.8% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet, and `weights.transforms()` resizes to 256, crops to 224 and normalises with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation.<Cite id="tv-models" />

```python
import torch
from torch import nn
from torchvision.models import resnet18, ResNet18_Weights

weights = ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT
model = resnet18(weights=weights)            # downloads ~45 MB once
preprocess = weights.transforms()            # the preprocessing the weights expect

for p in model.parameters():                 # 1. freeze the pretrained body
    p.requires_grad = False
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, 3)    # 2. new head: 512 -> 3 classes
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3, momentum=0.9)  # 3. train the head
```

The [official PyTorch tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/transfer_learning_tutorial.html) uses this setup to classify ants and bees from about 120 training images per class, a dataset far too small to train a ResNet from scratch.<Cite id="pt-transfer" /> It compares two scenarios. In **fine-tuning**, all layers are trained, starting from the pretrained weights. In **feature extraction**, the body is frozen and only the new final layer learns; the tutorial notes that this takes about half the time on a CPU, since gradients are not needed for most of the network.<Cite id="pt-transfer" />

## Feature extraction or full fine-tuning?

| Situation | Usual choice |
|---|---|
| Little data, similar to the pretraining images | Freeze the body, train a new head |
| More data, or a somewhat different domain | Fine-tune all layers, with a small learning rate for the body |
| Very different inputs (X-rays, sketches, spectrograms) | Fine-tune more layers, or compare against training from scratch |

When fine-tuning everything, the pretrained layers get a smaller learning rate than the new head. Dive into Deep Learning's hot-dog example trains the output layer with a learning rate ten times that of the pretrained layers, so that large, noisy gradients from the random head do not wreck useful features.<Cite id="d2l-fine-tuning" /> In its experiment the fine-tuned model beat an identical network trained from scratch.<Cite id="d2l-fine-tuning" />

<Callout type="pitfall">
`requires_grad = False` stops the weights from changing but not the <Term slug="batch-normalization" /> running statistics: in `model.train()` mode those keep updating from your batches.<Cite id="pt-batchnorm2d" /> To keep a frozen body truly fixed, put its BatchNorm layers in `eval()` mode.
</Callout>

## When transfer does not help

Transfer can fail, or even hurt, when the source and target tasks are too different; the literature calls this *negative transfer*.<Cite id="wiki-transfer" /> Practical mismatches matter too. ImageNet models expect 224×224 colour photos, so 28×28 grayscale digits would have to be enlarged and copied into three channels, and on MNIST a small CNN trained from scratch already reaches about 99%. The projects in this course therefore train their own small networks.

## Measured on the course's own models

The handwriting project trains a digits CNN first and then [a letters CNN](/course/project-digits-letters/emnist-letters/) with the same architecture. That makes digits-to-letters a clean small experiment: `transfer_digits_to_letters.py` loads the trained [digits model](/course/project-digits-letters/mnist-cnn/), keeps everything except the 10-class output layer, and compares three strategies on the same small set of letters.

With 2,000 training letters, 10 epochs and 5,000 test letters, one CPU run printed:

| Strategy | Trainable parameters | Test accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| From scratch | 423,706 | 81.5% |
| Frozen conv layers (feature extraction) | 404,890 | 82.6% |
| Fine-tune all, body at 1e-4 | 423,706 | 83.5% |

The gain is real but small, and with only 500 training letters transfer did not help at all (62.1% from scratch against 57.5-58.0%). The digits network is shallow, its two convolutional layers learn generic strokes that a network also learns quickly from scratch, and digits are a narrow source task. This matches Yosinski and colleagues' finding that transferability depends on depth and on how related the tasks are.<Cite id="yosinski2014" /> Large gains come from deep networks pretrained on millions of varied images. Run the script with `--train-size 500` to see the small-data case yourself; numbers vary slightly between machines.

## Summary

- Transfer learning starts from pretrained weights; fine-tuning adapts them to a new task.
- Early layers learn general features and later layers task-specific ones, so the head is replaced and the body reused.
- Feature extraction freezes the body and trains a tiny head; full fine-tuning trains everything with a small learning rate for the body.
- Transfer helps most with little data and a large, broadly pretrained source model; on the course's shallow digits CNN the gain is 1-2 points.

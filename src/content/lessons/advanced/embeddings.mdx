An **embedding** is a short list of learned numbers that stands for a discrete symbol such as a character, a word or a code token.<Cite id="wiki-word-embedding" /> Neural networks can only multiply and add numbers, so every text model starts by turning each token into a vector; embeddings do this in a way that lets similar symbols get similar vectors.<Cite id="bengio2003" /> They are the first layer of every model in the [small-text](/course/project-text/names-mlp/) and [code autocompletion](/course/project-code-autocomplete/code-tiny-gpt/) projects.

## From one-hot vectors to embeddings

The simplest way to feed a symbol to a network is a <Term slug="one-hot-encoding">one-hot vector</Term>: a list as long as the vocabulary, with a 1 at the symbol's position and 0 everywhere else. One-hot vectors have two problems. They are long (50,000 numbers for a 50,000-word vocabulary) and they say nothing about meaning, because every pair of one-hot vectors is exactly as far apart as every other pair.<Cite id="wiki-word-embedding" />

An embedding layer fixes both. It keeps a table $C$ with one row per symbol and a small number of columns, for example 10. Looking up a symbol returns its row. PyTorch describes [`nn.Embedding`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Embedding.html) as exactly this: a lookup table indexed by integer ids, whose rows are ordinary trainable weights.<Cite id="pt-embedding" />

    { symbol: 'V', meaning: 'vocabulary size' },
    { symbol: 'd', meaning: 'embedding size' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="C = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.3 \\ 0.7 & -0.2 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \\ -0.4 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}" words="A table for a 4-symbol vocabulary with 2 numbers per symbol: V = 4 rows, d = 2 columns." />
  <Step tex="e_2 = [0,\ 0,\ \hl{1},\ 0]" words="The one-hot vector for symbol 2 (counting from 0)." />
  <Step tex="e_2\, C = 0 \cdot C_0 + 0 \cdot C_1 + \hl{1 \cdot C_2} + 0 \cdot C_3 = [0.5,\ 0.5]" words="Multiplying adds up the rows, each weighted by the one-hot entry, so only row 2 survives." />
  <Step tex="\text{emb}(2) = C[\hl{2}] = [0.5,\ 0.5]" words="Indexing gives the same row without the multiplications. This is what nn.Embedding does, and why it is fast." />
</MathSteps>

Because the lookup equals a matrix product, the gradient flows into the table like into any other weight matrix: each training step nudges only the rows of the symbols that appeared in the batch.<Cite id="pt-embedding" />

<Analogy breaks="Real map pins have two coordinates; embeddings have 10 to several hundred, and their axes have no names.">
Think of each symbol as a pin on a map. Training slides the pins around until symbols that are used in similar ways sit near each other.
</Analogy>

## The Bengio-style MLP language model

A <Term slug="language-model" /> predicts the next token from the ones before it, $P(x_t \mid x_{t-n+1}, \dots, x_{t-1})$; multiplying these predictions gives the probability of a whole sequence.<Cite id="d2l-lm" /> Counting models (<Term slug="n-gram">n-grams</Term>) estimate each probability from a table of counts, which explodes in size and cannot handle a context it has never seen.<Cite id="d2l-lm" />

In 2003 Yoshua Bengio and colleagues proposed a neural alternative. Each word gets a learned feature vector; the vectors of the previous $n-1$ words are concatenated, passed through a <Term slug="tanh" /> hidden layer, and a <Term slug="softmax" /> turns the output scores into next-word probabilities.<Cite id="bengio2003" /> Because similar words end up with similar vectors, a sentence seen in training also makes similar unseen sentences more likely. The paper calls this fighting the *curse of dimensionality*.<Cite id="bengio2003" />

Andrej Karpathy's [makemore](https://github.com/karpathy/makemore) rebuilds this model at the character level on a list of about 32,000 names. The vocabulary has 27 symbols: 26 letters plus a "." that marks the start and end of a name.<Cite id="makemore" />

Before training, the model should be about equally unsure of all 27 symbols, so its <Term slug="cross-entropy" /> loss should start near $\ln 27 \approx 3.30$.<Cite id="makemore" /> In the course project the trained model reaches a dev loss of about 2.14, against 2.45 for a counting bigram model.

## Measuring similarity

Once symbols are vectors, "similar" can be measured. The <Term slug="dot-product" /> $a \cdot b$ multiplies matching entries and adds them up; it is large when the vectors point the same way, but it also grows with their lengths. <Term slug="cosine-similarity" /> divides the dot product by both lengths, leaving only the angle: 1 for the same direction, 0 for perpendicular, −1 for opposite.<Cite id="wiki-word-embedding" />

<MathSteps
  title="Dot product and cosine for three embeddings"
  legend={[
    { symbol: 'a, b', meaning: 'two embedding vectors' },
    { symbol: '\\lVert a \\rVert', meaning: 'length of a, the square root of a · a' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="a = [0.5, 1.0, -0.5],\ \ b = [0.4, 0.9, -0.3],\ \ z = [-0.6, 0.1, 0.8]" words="Three made-up embeddings: a and b are meant to be similar symbols, z a different one." />
  <Step tex="a \cdot b = 0.2 + 0.9 + 0.15 = \hl{1.25}, \quad a \cdot z = -0.3 + 0.1 - 0.4 = \hl{-0.6}" words="Multiply matching entries and add. Positive means 'roughly the same direction'." />
  <Step tex="\lVert a \rVert \approx 1.225,\ \ \lVert b \rVert \approx 1.030,\ \ \lVert z \rVert \approx 1.005" words="Each length is the square root of the vector's dot product with itself." />
  <Step tex="\cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert} \approx \frac{1.25}{1.261} \approx \hl{0.99}" words="Dividing by both lengths keeps only the direction: a and b point almost the same way." />
  <Step tex="\cos(a, z) \approx \frac{-0.6}{1.231} \approx \hl{-0.49}" words="a and z point in fairly different directions." />
</MathSteps>

Attention, covered in [Attention, step by step](/course/advanced/attention/), uses exactly these dot products to decide which tokens should listen to which.

## Word2vec and the geometry of meaning

In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google showed that embeddings can be learned cheaply from huge text collections with two shallow models, **CBOW** (predict a word from its neighbours) and **skip-gram** (predict the neighbours from a word), together known as <Term slug="word2vec" />.<Cite id="mikolov2013" /> They reported that simple vector arithmetic captures relationships: the vector for "King" minus "Man" plus "Woman" lands closest to "Queen".<Cite id="mikolov2013" />

The idea behind all of this is the *distributional hypothesis*: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.<Cite id="wiki-word-embedding" /> Modern language models no longer use separate word2vec vectors; they learn their embedding table together with the rest of the network, as the Bengio model already did.<Cite id="d2l-lm" />

<Callout type="note">
Vector arithmetic on embeddings is a striking demonstration, not a guarantee. The famous analogies hold for some relations and fail for many others, and embeddings also absorb biases present in their training text.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An embedding is a learned vector per symbol; the lookup equals a one-hot vector times the embedding matrix.
- Bengio et al. (2003) concatenated context embeddings, applied a tanh hidden layer and a softmax: the first neural language model.
- The character version used in the names project has $270 + 6{,}200 + 5{,}427 = 11{,}897$ parameters and starts at a loss near $\ln 27 \approx 3.30$.
- The dot product and cosine similarity measure how alike two embeddings are; cosine ignores length.
- Word2vec showed that embeddings learned from large text capture some relationships as directions.

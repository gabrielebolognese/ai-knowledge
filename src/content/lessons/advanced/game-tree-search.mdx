**Game-tree search** is the method computer programs use to choose moves in two-player games such as tic-tac-toe, checkers and chess: imagine every possible move, every reply to it, and so on, then pick the move whose worst case is best.<Cite id="cpw-minimax" /> Its basic rule, **minimax**, assumes both players always play their best. **Alpha-beta pruning** returns exactly the same answer while skipping branches that cannot change it, which is why it powers the search of the chess engine built later in the course.<Cite id="cpw-alphabeta" />

## Search and evaluation

A position is the root of a <Term slug="game-tree" />. Each legal move is a branch to a child position, and each child has its own branches for the opponent's replies.<Cite id="wiki-minimax" /> One move by one player is called a *ply*. In 1950 Claude Shannon described how a chess program could search such a tree to a fixed depth and score the positions at the bottom with an evaluation function.<Cite id="shannon1950" /> Those two parts, search and evaluation, still structure every chess engine.

The tree grows fast. With a branching factor $b$ of about 35 legal moves in a chess middlegame and a depth of $d$ plies, a full search visits about $b^d$ positions: $35^4 \approx 1.5$ million, and $35^6 \approx 1.8$ billion.<Cite id="cpw-minimax" /> Even tic-tac-toe has a full game tree of 549,946 positions when searched from the empty board, as the script at the end of this lesson counts.

<Analogy breaks="A hiker's gut feeling and lookahead are not separate machines; in an engine they are two different functions, and either can be improved without the other.">
Search is a hiker looking ahead at forks in the path; evaluation is the hiker's gut feeling about the view from any one spot. A better gut feeling means fewer paths must be walked to choose well.
</Analogy>

## Minimax

<Term slug="minimax">Minimax</Term> labels the levels of the tree. At MAX levels, the player to move picks the child with the highest value; at MIN levels, the opponent picks the lowest. Values flow up from the leaves, which are scored by the evaluation function or by the game result.<Cite id="cpw-minimax" /> The name comes from game theory, where John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for zero-sum games in 1928.<Cite id="wiki-minimax" />

Take a root where MAX has three moves, a, b and c. Each leads to a MIN node with three leaves: a has $[4, 6, 5]$, b has $[7, 2, 9]$ and c has $[1, 8, 3]$.

  <Step tex="V(a) = \min(4, 6, 5) = \hl{4}" words="At a MIN node the opponent picks the reply that is worst for MAX." />
  <Step tex="V(b) = \min(7, 2, 9) = \hl{2}, \quad V(c) = \min(1, 8, 3) = \hl{1}" words="Do the same for the other two moves. Move b has a tempting 9, but the opponent will answer with 2." />
  <Step tex="V(\text{root}) = \max(4, 2, 1) = \hl{4}" words="MAX picks the move with the best guaranteed outcome, so the answer is move a, worth 4." />
  <Step tex="\max(a, b) = -\min(-a, -b)" words="This identity lets one function play both sides: a minimum is a maximum of negated values." />
  <Step tex="V(p) = \max_{m} \big(\hl{-}V(p \text{ after } m)\big)" words="This is negamax. Every value is scored for the side to move, and the sign flips at each ply, because a position worth +3 for the opponent is worth −3 for me." />
</MathSteps>

**Negamax** is simply this compact form of minimax. It needs one condition: the evaluation must return scores from the point of view of the side to move.<Cite id="cpw-negamax" /> The chess project's evaluator, and later its neural network, are built that way for this reason.

## Alpha-beta pruning

<Term slug="alpha-beta-pruning">Alpha-beta</Term> carries two bounds down the tree. **Alpha** is the score the maximising player is already guaranteed elsewhere; **beta** is the most the opponent will allow, because they already have a better option elsewhere.<Cite id="cpw-alphabeta" /> As soon as a branch is proven to be no better than alpha for MAX, the rest of it can be skipped: whatever is in it, MAX will not choose it.<Cite id="wiki-alphabeta" /> Knuth and Moore published the classic analysis of the method in 1975.<Cite id="knuth1975" />

<Analogy breaks="Flight prices are known exactly once quoted; in a game tree the 'price' of a branch is itself the result of a search further down.">
You are shopping for the cheapest flight and already have one for €100. The next airline's first quote is €140 and its fares can only be at least that, so you close its website without reading the rest.
</Analogy>

</MathSteps>

## Why move ordering matters

Alpha-beta always returns the minimax value, but how much it saves depends on the order in which moves are tried, called <Term slug="move-ordering" />.<Cite id="cpw-alphabeta" /> If the same tree is searched in the order c, b, a, alpha starts at 1, then rises to 2, and no leaf is ever low enough to cut: all 9 leaves are evaluated. If, instead, the best move comes first and each refutation is the first reply tried, only 5 leaves are needed.

<MathSteps
  title="Best and worst cases (Knuth and Moore)"
  legend={[
    { symbol: 'b', meaning: 'branching factor' },
    { symbol: 'd', meaning: 'search depth in plies' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="\text{worst order: } b^{d}" words="With the worst ordering nothing is pruned, and alpha-beta visits as many leaves as minimax." />
  <Step tex="\text{best order: } b^{\lceil d/2 \rceil} + b^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} - 1" words="With perfect ordering, Knuth and Moore showed this minimum number of leaves." example="our tree: 3 + 3 − 1 = 5" />
  <Step tex="b = 40,\ d = 6:\quad 40^6 = 4{,}096{,}000{,}000 \;\to\; 40^3 + 40^3 - 1 = \hl{127{,}999}" words="For a chess-like tree, perfect ordering cuts four billion leaves to about 128 thousand." />
  <Step tex="b^{d/2} = \sqrt{b^{d}}" words="The best case is roughly the square root of the full tree, so in the same time the search can reach about twice the depth." />
</MathSteps>

This is the formula from Knuth and Moore's analysis, and the Chessprogramming Wiki works the same $b = 40$, $d = 6$ example.<Cite id="knuth1975" /><Cite id="cpw-alphabeta" /> Perfect ordering is impossible, since knowing the best move first is the whole problem, but good guesses get close: try captures of valuable pieces first, and the move that was best in a shallower search.

## Summary

- Game-tree search looks ahead through moves and replies; a full search costs about $b^d$ positions.
- Minimax backs up values from the leaves: MAX takes the highest child, MIN the lowest. Negamax writes this once by flipping the sign at each ply, with side-to-move scores.
- Alpha-beta keeps a window $[\alpha, \beta]$ and skips branches that cannot change the result; the answer equals minimax.
- Move ordering decides the savings: from none in the worst order to about $\sqrt{b^d}$ leaves in the best.

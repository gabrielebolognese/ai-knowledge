**Attention** is a way for each position in a sequence to gather information from other positions, by taking a weighted average of their vectors where the weights say how relevant each position is right now.<Cite id="d2l-attention" /> It was introduced to fix a bottleneck in machine translation and became the only mixing operation of the Transformer, the architecture behind GPT and the [code autocompletion project](/course/project-code-autocomplete/code-tiny-gpt/).<Cite id="vaswani2017" />

## The bottleneck that attention removed

Early neural translators used two <Term slug="recurrent-neural-network">RNNs</Term>: an encoder read the whole source sentence into one fixed vector, and a decoder produced the translation from that vector alone.<Cite id="sutskever2014" /> One vector has to hold everything, so quality dropped on long sentences.<Cite id="bahdanau2014" />

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) let the decoder look back at every encoder state at every output step. It scores how well each source position matches what it is about to produce, turns the scores into weights with a <Term slug="softmax" />, and uses the weighted average, the *context vector*, as extra input.<Cite id="bahdanau2014" /> The weights act as a soft alignment between source and target words and can be drawn as a heat map.<Cite id="distill-attention" /> Bahdanau's version scored with a small neural network; Luong, Pham and Manning (2015) showed that a plain <Term slug="dot-product" /> also works well and is cheaper.<Cite id="luong2015" />

## Queries, keys and values

Vaswani et al. (2017) generalised the idea. Each token's vector $x$ is projected three ways by learned matrices: a **query** $q = xW_Q$ ("what am I looking for?"), a **key** $k = xW_K$ ("what do I contain?") and a **value** $v = xW_V$ ("what I hand over if chosen").<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> The output for a token is a sum of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key. When queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence, this is <Term slug="self-attention" />. The <Term slug="query-key-value" /> naming borrows from information retrieval.<Cite id="d2l-attention" />

<Analogy breaks="In a real library you pick one book; attention always takes a blend of all of them, and the 'questions' and 'catalogue cards' are learned vectors no human wrote.">
Your query is the question you type into a library catalogue; every book has a catalogue card (its key) and contents (its value). You take a mix of all the contents, weighted by how well each card matches your question.
</Analogy>

## Scaled dot-product attention by hand

The formula is $\text{softmax}(QK^\top / \sqrt{d_k})\,V$, where $d_k$ is the size of the keys.<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> Here it is for the third of three tokens, with $d_k = 4$.

In matrix form every token does this at once: $QK^\top$ is a $T \times T$ table of scores, one row per query, and each row goes through its own softmax.<Cite id="d2l-attention" /> PyTorch provides the whole computation as [`F.scaled_dot_product_attention`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention.html).<Cite id="pt-sdpa" />

## Why divide by √d

If the components of $q$ and $k$ are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product is a sum of $d_k$ such products and has variance $d_k$.<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> For $d_k = 64$ the typical score is then about 8 in size. Softmax of large scores is nearly one-hot: scores $[8, 0, 0]$ give weights $[0.9993, 0.0003, 0.0003]$, while $[1, 0, 0]$ gives $[0.58, 0.21, 0.21]$. A nearly one-hot softmax has almost no gradient, so learning stalls. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the variance back to 1.<Cite id="vaswani2017" />

## The causal mask

A model that generates text left to right must not peek at the tokens it is trying to predict. The <Term slug="causal-mask" /> sets every score for a later position to minus infinity before the softmax, so those weights become exactly zero.<Cite id="vaswani2017" />

With the mask, a single training sequence of $T$ tokens gives $T$ next-token predictions at once, each using only its past.

## Multi-head attention and cost

Instead of one attention with large vectors, the Transformer runs $h$ smaller ones in parallel, each on its own learned projection of size $d_{model}/h$, then concatenates the results and mixes them with one more matrix $W_O$.<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> The base model of the 2017 paper used 8 heads of size 64 on 512-number vectors. Different heads can learn different patterns, such as "look at the previous token" or "look at the matching bracket". This is <Term slug="multi-head-attention" />.<Cite id="d2l-attention" />

The score table has $T \times T$ entries, so time and memory grow with the square of the context length. That is one reason the course's CPU models use contexts of 64 to 128 tokens.<Cite id="d2l-attention" />

<Callout type="warning">
Attention weights are tempting to read as explanations ("the model looked here"). Jain and Wallace found that different weight patterns can give the same predictions, so treat heat maps as one clue, not as proof of the model's reasoning.<Cite id="jain2019" />
</Callout>

## Summary

- Attention replaces a fixed memory with a weighted average over all positions (Bahdanau et al. 2014).
- Scaled dot-product attention: score $q \cdot k$, divide by $\sqrt{d_k}$, softmax, then mix the values.
- Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the score variance near 1 so the softmax does not saturate.
- A causal mask sets future scores to minus infinity, so a language model cannot copy the answer.
- Multi-head attention runs several smaller attentions in parallel; the cost grows with the square of the context length.

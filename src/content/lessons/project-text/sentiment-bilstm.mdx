A **bidirectional LSTM classifier** reads a sentence word by word, once from left to right and once from right to left, and classifies it from the two final memories. Unlike the bag of embeddings, it sees word order, so it can in principle learn that "not" changes the word after it. This capstone trains it on SST-2, compares it honestly with the baseline, and wraps it in `classify.py` so you can test your own sentences.

## Reading in both directions

The previous lesson's <Term slug="long-short-term-memory">LSTM</Term> read text in one direction.<Cite id="hochreiter1997" /> For classification the whole sentence is available at once, so there is no reason to read only forwards. A bidirectional network runs two independent recurrent layers, one over the words in order and one in reverse, and combines their results; Schuster and Paliwal introduced the idea in 1997.<Cite id="schuster1997" />

<Analogy breaks="A person rereads with understanding; the two LSTMs never exchange information while reading, only their final states are combined.">
It is like reading a sentence once normally and once backwards and then deciding: the first reading ends knowing how the sentence finished, the second ends knowing how it began.
</Analogy>

The forward LSTM's last state summarises the sentence ending at its last word; the backward LSTM's last state summarises it ending at its first word. Line 40 joins the two 128-number states into 256 numbers, and a linear layer produces the two scores. Dropout (30%) on the embeddings and on the joined state reduces overfitting.

### Packing: keep padding out

Sentences in a batch are padded to the same length. If the padded matrix went straight into the LSTM, the backward direction would start in the padding and read many empty tokens before the real words. `pack_padded_sequence` (line 38) passes each sentence's true length, so both directions start and stop at real words.<Cite id="pt-pack" /> `enforce_sorted=False` lets the batch keep its original order.

## Results: an honest comparison

`python train_classifier.py --model bilstm` trains for four epochs with Adam at a learning rate of 0.002 and keeps the best epoch by validation accuracy. On the test machine (Windows 11 laptop CPU, PyTorch 2.15 CPU build, seed 0) it took 682 seconds, about 11 minutes, while other training jobs shared the CPU:

| Epoch | Train loss | Validation accuracy (carved) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.556 | 83.4% |
| 2 | 0.356 | 88.5% |
| 3 | 0.271 | **90.4%** |

Side by side with the baseline, on the same data and test sentences:

| | Bag of embeddings | BiLSTM |
|---|---|---|
| Parameters | 941,698 | 1,140,738 |
| Training time (test machine) | 96 s | 682 s |
| **Test accuracy** (872 GLUE sentences) | 78.67% | **83.14%** |
| Precision / recall, positive class | 74.2% / 89.2% | 80.5% / 88.3% |
| False positives / misses | 138 / 48 | 95 / 52 |
| Challenge set (48 sentences) | 60.4% (29) | 64.6% (31) |
| UCI Amazon/Yelp/IMDb (3,000) | 79.1% | 81.2% |

The BiLSTM gains 4.5 points of test accuracy, almost all of it from fewer false positives: 95 negative sentences called positive instead of 138. That fits the error analysis of the previous lesson, where negative reviews full of positive words fooled the average. It also transfers slightly better to the other review domains. It costs about seven times more training time.

### Negation: better, not solved

On the 48 challenge sentences the BiLSTM gets 31 right against 29 for the bag. With so few sentences, two extra correct answers are within noise, so the honest conclusion is "slightly better at most". Looking at individual sentences is more informative:

- It now handles **negated praise**: "not good" is negative (85.4%) and "the film is not good" is negative (91.4%), where the bag said positive.
- It still fails **negated criticism**: "not bad", "it was not bad", "i did not hate this movie" and "the story is never boring" are all called negative.

The second group needs the model to learn that negating a negative word gives a mild positive, and the training data rarely shows that. Of the 63,982 training items, 5,594 contain "not", "n't", "never" or "no", and 68% of those are negative. Phrases such as "not bad", "not dull" or "never boring" occur only about 30 times in total. The architecture can represent order; the data barely teaches this pattern. Socher et al. evaluated exactly these two cases, negated positive and negated negative sentences, and reported that a tree-structured model trained on the treebank's phrase-level labels captured negation better than bag-of-words baselines.<Cite id="socher2013" />

## What scale buys

Both models here learn everything from about 64,000 labelled phrases. The GLUE paper's own baselines, trained without large-scale pre-training, also land in the 80s on SST-2.<Cite id="wang2018" /> Fine-tuned pre-trained Transformers do far better: BERT reported 93.5% (base) and 94.9% (large) on the SST-2 test set.<Cite id="devlin2018" /> The difference is mostly the billions of words of unlabelled text BERT read before it ever saw a sentiment label, from which it learned what words mean and how they combine.

## Classifying your own sentences

`train_classifier.py` saves the weights, the vocabulary and the configuration together in one checkpoint, so `classify.py` can rebuild exactly the same model and tokenizer.<Cite id="pt-save" />

<CodeFile path="code/projects/project-text/classify.py" lines="19-35" highlight="23,24,25,31,32,33,34" title="classify.py: rebuild the model and score one sentence" />

The sentence goes through the same tokenizer and vocabulary as in training. Softmax turns the two scores into a probability, and any word mapped to `<unk>` is reported, because the model knows nothing about it.

```bash
python classify.py "a warm, funny and clever film" "not good at all" "the jokes never miss"
python classify.py --model checkpoints/bag.pt "not bad at all"     # compare with the baseline
python classify.py                                                 # interactive: type, Enter, empty line to quit
```

On the test machine the BiLSTM and the bag of embeddings answered:

| Sentence | BiLSTM | Bag of embeddings |
|---|---|---|
| not good | negative 85.4% | negative 65.7% |
| the film is not good | negative 91.4% | positive 62.9% |
| not bad | negative 96.1% | negative 100.0% |
| the jokes never miss | negative 79.5% | negative 75.1% |
| i laughed the whole time | positive 95.4% | positive 99.8% |
| the cinematography was gorgeous but the plot was a total mess | negative 97.8% | negative 98.4% |

Treat the probability as the model's confidence, not as a calibrated chance of being right: small networks trained with cross-entropy are often overconfident on unusual inputs, especially those full of unknown words.

## Summary

- A BiLSTM reads each sentence in both directions and classifies from the two final states; packing keeps padding out of both directions.
- It has 1,140,738 parameters, 83% of them still in the word vectors.
- On SST-2 it reaches 83.14% test accuracy against 78.67% for the bag of embeddings, mainly by making fewer false-positive errors, at about seven times the training time.
- Saving weights, vocabulary and configuration together lets `classify.py` rebuild the exact model and score any sentence, reporting unknown words.
- Small models trained from scratch stay around 80% on SST-2; pre-trained Transformers exceed 93% because of what they learned from unlabelled text.

**SST-2** is a dataset of short movie-review texts, each labelled positive or negative, and a standard benchmark for <Term slug="sentiment-analysis" />. It is the binary version of the Stanford Sentiment Treebank and one of the tasks of the GLUE benchmark.<Cite id="socher2013" /><Cite id="wang2018" /> This lesson downloads it, cuts sentences into words, builds a vocabulary and pads sentences of different lengths into batches, the preparation that both classifiers in this module rely on.

## What is in SST-2

Socher et al. took sentences from movie reviews on the Rotten Tomatoes website, parsed each one into a tree of phrases and had people rate the sentiment of every phrase.<Cite id="socher2013" /> GLUE packages the binary version, with neutral items removed, as a zip file of tab-separated `sentence<TAB>label` lines, where 1 means positive.<Cite id="glue" /><Cite id="sst2-zip" />

The test labels are kept secret for the benchmark leaderboard, so this course uses GLUE's 872 validation sentences as its test set, and carves its own validation set out of the training data.<Cite id="wang2018" /> The training items are short: half of them have 7 tokens or fewer, because many are phrases such as "dry humor and jarring shocks". The test sentences are whole sentences, with a median of 19 tokens.

### Licences: what may be shipped

SST-2 does not state a licence: the Stanford page asks users to cite the paper, and the Hugging Face card lists the licence as unknown.<Cite id="hf-sst2" /> The course therefore never redistributes it. `sst2_data.py` downloads the zip into `data/`, which git ignores.

Two small sets are bundled in `bundled/`, each with its licence recorded in `bundled/LICENSES.md`:

- **Sentiment Labelled Sentences** by Kotzias et al.: 3,000 review sentences from Amazon, Yelp and IMDb, published by the UCI repository under CC BY 4.0, which allows redistribution with credit.<Cite id="kotzias2015" /> The smoke test trains on it offline, and the full run uses it as a test from other domains.
- **`challenge.tsv`**: 48 sentences written for this course (released under CC0) that test negation and contrast, such as "not bad at all".

## From sentences to tokens

A classifier works with numbers, so each sentence is first split into *tokens* (here, words and punctuation marks) and each token is mapped to an id. SST-2 already comes lowercased and split, with "it's" written as "it 's" and "isn't" as "is n't". The tokenizer applies the same rules to anything a user types, so "I didn't like it, it's dull!" becomes `i did n't like it , it 's dull !`.

Matching the dataset's conventions matters more than a clever tokenizer. If the model learned "n't" as a token during training, user input must produce "n't" too, or the most important negation word would become unknown.

## A vocabulary from the training split only

The vocabulary lists every distinct token in the training data, most frequent first, after two special entries: `<pad>` (id 0) fills empty positions and `<unk>` (id 1) stands for any word not in the list.

<CodeFile path="code/projects/project-text/sst2_data.py" lines="75-92" highlight="83,84,85,86,92" title="sst2_data.py: Vocab, built from training sentences" />

On SST-2 the vocabulary has 14,712 entries, and the most common tokens are "the", ",", "a", "and" and "of". Each id later selects one row of an embedding table, a trainable vector per word.<Cite id="pt-embedding" />

<Analogy breaks="A real dictionary can be extended at any time; here the list is frozen once training starts, and any new word is mapped to one shared row.">
The vocabulary is like a dictionary printed before the exam: words that were not in it on printing day all point to the same entry, "unknown word".
</Analogy>

Building the list only from training sentences is deliberate. In real use, people type words the model never saw, and those words become `<unk>`. On the SST-2 test sentences, 5.6% of tokens are unknown, which is an honest preview of that problem.

## Padding and batching

## Three splits, and one trap

`split_off` shuffles the 67,349 training items and moves 5% into a validation set, giving 63,982 for training and 3,367 for validation. The positive share is 55.8% in training and 50.9% in the test sentences, so a model that always said "positive" would score about 51% on the test.

The trap is in how SST-2 was built. Its training data contains the labelled phrases of each parsed sentence, not just whole sentences.<Cite id="socher2013" /> A phrase moved into the carved validation set usually still has its parent sentence in training: on the test machine, 89% of validation items appear word for word inside some training item. Validation accuracy is therefore optimistic. The course uses it only to pick the best epoch and reports the GLUE sentences as the test score.

## Summary

- SST-2 has 67,349 labelled training phrases and 872 validation sentences from movie reviews; the validation set serves as the test set here.
- SST-2 states no licence, so it is downloaded and cited, never redistributed. The bundled UCI sentences (CC BY 4.0) and the course's own challenge set (CC0) are committed with their licences.
- Tokens follow SST-2's conventions ("is n't", "it 's"), and the 14,712-word vocabulary comes from training data only, with `<pad>` and `<unk>`.
- Batches pad to the longest sentence and carry the true lengths, so models can mask the padding.
- A validation set carved from SST-2's training phrases overlaps heavily with training; use it to choose epochs, not to report results.

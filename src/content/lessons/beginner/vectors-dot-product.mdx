A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers, such as the three features of a house or the 784 pixel brightnesses of a small image. The **dot product** of two vectors of the same length multiplies them position by position and adds up the products, giving a single number.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-linalg" /> It is the most common calculation in deep learning: every <Term slug="artificial-neuron">artificial neuron</Term> computes one, and attention in Transformers compares words with dot products.<Cite id="d2l-linalg" />

## Two ways to see a vector

**As a list.** In code a <Term slug="vector" /> is a flat NumPy array, for example `np.array([2, 1, 3])`. The order matters: position 1 always means the same feature. The number of entries is the vector's *dimension*.<Cite id="d2l-linalg" />

**As an arrow.** A vector with two entries, such as $[3, 4]$, can be drawn as an arrow from the origin to the point 3 steps right and 4 steps up. Three entries give an arrow in 3-D space. Grant Sanderson's *Essence of linear algebra* builds the whole subject on this picture.<Cite id="3b1b-linalg" />

Vectors in machine learning usually have far more than three entries. The algebra is the same for 784 dimensions as for 2; only the drawing stops working.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-linalg" />

### Adding and scaling vectors

Two vectors of the same length are added entry by entry: $[3, 1] + [1, 2] = [4, 3]$. As arrows, this means walking along the first arrow and then along the second; the sum is the arrow from the start to where you end up.<Cite id="3b1b-linalg" />

Multiplying a vector by a single number, called *scaling*, multiplies every entry: $2 \cdot [3, 1] = [6, 2]$. The arrow keeps its direction and becomes twice as long. A negative number flips it to point the other way.<Cite id="khan-linalg" />

```python
import numpy as np

a = np.array([3, 1])
b = np.array([1, 2])
print(a + b)    # [4 3]
print(2 * a)    # [6 2]
print(a - b)    # [ 2 -1]
```

These two operations are all that gradient descent does to a model's weights: it scales the gradient vector by the learning rate and subtracts it from the weight vector.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-linalg" />

## The dot product as a shopping bill

Start with numbers. You buy 2 apples, 1 loaf of bread and 3 bottles of milk, priced at 0.5, 2 and 1 each. The bill is $2 \cdot 0.5 + 1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 1 = 6$. That calculation is exactly a dot product of the quantity vector and the price vector.

<Analogy breaks="Prices are never negative, but the weights of a neuron often are: a negative weight lowers the total, like a discount voucher.">
The dot product is a shopping bill: one vector lists how many of each item you bought, the other lists the price of each item, and the dot product is the total you pay.
</Analogy>

A neuron does the same thing with different names: the inputs play the role of quantities, the <Term slug="weight">weights</Term> play the role of prices, and the result is the weighted sum $z$ before the <Term slug="bias" /> is added.<Cite id="wiki-dot" /> This is why the [artificial neuron](/course/beginner/artificial-neuron/) lesson calls the weighted sum "a dot product plus a bias".

## The dot product in NumPy

NumPy offers three equivalent ways to compute a dot product for 1-D arrays: `np.dot(a, b)`, `a.dot(b)` and the `@` operator.<Cite id="numpy-dot" />

```python
import numpy as np

quantities = np.array([2, 1, 3])        # apples, bread, milk
prices = np.array([0.5, 2.0, 1.0])

print(quantities * prices)    # [1. 2. 3.]  element-wise, NOT a dot product
print(np.dot(quantities, prices))   # 6.0
print(quantities @ prices)          # 6.0
```

The first `print` is a common trap. The `*` operator multiplies position by position and keeps all three products; the dot product also adds them up. Writing `(quantities * prices).sum()` would give the same 6.0, but `@` does it in one step.<Cite id="numpy-dot" />

## Length of a vector

The length of an arrow, called its **norm** and written $\|v\|$, comes from Pythagoras' theorem.<Cite id="khan-linalg" /> The <Term slug="vector-norm">norm</Term> reappears later in weight decay and in normalising embeddings.

## The dot product as a similarity score

The dot product also has a geometric meaning. For two arrows with angle $\theta$ between them, $a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\theta$.<Cite id="wiki-dot" /> You do not need trigonometry to use this: $\cos\theta$ is simply a number from $-1$ to $1$ that measures how well the two arrows line up.

| Arrows | Angle | $\cos\theta$ | Sign of $a \cdot b$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same direction | 0° | 1 | positive, as large as possible |
| At a right angle | 90° | 0 | zero |
| Opposite directions | 180° | −1 | negative |

<Analogy breaks="People can agree on some points and disagree on others; the dot product boils that down to one number and loses the detail.">
Think of two arrows as two people's opinions. Pointing the same way, they agree (large positive dot product); at right angles, their views are unrelated (zero); pointing opposite ways, they disagree (negative).
</Analogy>

Dividing the dot product by both lengths removes the effect of size and leaves only the alignment. The result is the <Term slug="cosine-similarity" />, a standard way to compare word embeddings and search results.<Cite id="wiki-cosine" />

<MathSteps
  title="Cosine similarity of [1, 2] and [2, 1]"
  legend={[
    { symbol: 'a, b', meaning: 'the two vectors' },
    { symbol: '\\cos\\theta', meaning: 'alignment, from -1 to 1' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="a \cdot b = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = \hl{4}" words="Compute the dot product as before." />
  <Step tex="\|a\| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5}, \quad \|b\| = \sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt{5}" words="Compute each length. Both arrows happen to have length √5 ≈ 2.236." />
  <Step tex="\cos\theta = \frac{4}{\hl{\sqrt{5} \cdot \sqrt{5}}} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8" words="Divide the dot product by the product of the lengths. 0.8 is close to 1, so the arrows point in similar directions." />
  <Step tex="\cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\hl{\|a\|\,\|b\|}}" words="The general cosine-similarity formula." />
</MathSteps>

```python
def cosine(a, b):
    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

print(np.linalg.norm(np.array([3, 4])))           # 5.0
print(cosine(np.array([1, 0]), np.array([0, 5]))) # 0.0  (right angle)
print(cosine(np.array([1, 2]), np.array([2, 1]))) # 0.8 (prints 0.7999999999999998)
```

The last line prints a value a hair below 0.8 because computers store decimals with tiny rounding errors.<Cite id="numpy-dot" /> Later lessons use this similarity idea directly: embeddings place related words on arrows that point in similar directions, and self-attention scores how well a query vector matches each key vector with a dot product.<Cite id="d2l-linalg" />

<Callout type="pitfall">
"Vectors only exist in 2-D or 3-D." In machine learning a vector often has hundreds or thousands of entries. The formulas do not change; only the pictures do.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A vector is an ordered list of numbers and, equivalently, an arrow from the origin.
- The dot product $a \cdot b = \sum_i a_i b_i$ multiplies matching entries and adds them up.
- A neuron's weighted sum is a dot product of weights and inputs.
- The norm $\|v\| = \sqrt{v \cdot v}$ is the arrow's length.
- The sign and size of the dot product measure alignment; cosine similarity divides out the lengths.

An **artificial neuron** is the basic unit of a neural network: it takes several input numbers, multiplies each one by a <Term slug="weight" />, adds the results together with a <Term slug="bias" />, and passes that total through an <Term slug="activation-function">activation function</Term> to produce a single output.<Cite id="wiki-neuron" /> Stacking many of these units in layers gives the networks used for image, text and game-playing models.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch6" />

## What a neuron computes

A neuron does two things in order.<Cite id="cs231n-nn1" /> First it computes a **weighted sum**, written $z$: each input $x_i$ is multiplied by its weight $w_i$, the products are added up, and the bias $b$ is added at the end. Adding up products position by position is called a <Term slug="dot-product" />, so the weighted sum is "the dot product of the weights and the inputs, plus the bias".

Second, the neuron applies an activation function $\phi$ ("phi") to $z$. The result $a = \phi(z)$ is the neuron's output, which can become an input to the next layer.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch6" />

<Analogy breaks="A real committee member can change their mind for reasons outside the evidence; a neuron's output is fixed by its numbers.">
Picture a committee member deciding whether to speak up. Each input is a piece of evidence, each weight is how much they trust that evidence (a negative weight means distrust), and the bias is their mood before the meeting. The activation decides whether the total is enough to say something.
</Analogy>

The weights and the bias are the neuron's **parameters**. They are not written by hand in a real network: training adjusts them automatically, as the lesson on [gradient descent](/course/beginner/gradient-descent/) shows.<Cite id="d2l-mlp" />

## Where the idea comes from

The name is borrowed from biology, but only loosely. In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts proposed a simplified model of a nerve cell that adds up incoming signals and "fires" when the total crosses a threshold; that threshold unit is the ancestor of the neuron described here.<Cite id="wiki-neuron" /> A biological neuron communicates with timed electrical spikes and chemical signals, while an artificial one just outputs a number, so the similarity is an inspiration rather than a description of the brain.<Cite id="cs231n-nn1" />

What matters for machine learning is the arithmetic. Because a neuron is built only from multiplications, additions and a simple function, a computer can evaluate millions of them per second, and the same small recipe works whether the inputs are pixel brightnesses, letters of text or features of a chess position.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch6" />

## Activation functions

Without an activation, a neuron only computes a straight-line (linear) function of its inputs, and stacking linear layers still gives a linear function. The non-linear activation is what lets deeper networks represent curved decision boundaries.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch6" />

| Activation | Formula | Output range | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Step | 1 if $z > 0$, else 0 | 0 or 1 | The original perceptron |
| <Term slug="sigmoid">Sigmoid</Term> | $1 / (1 + e^{-z})$ | 0 to 1 | Yes/no probabilities |
| <Term slug="relu">ReLU</Term> | $\max(0, z)$ | 0 and up | Default for hidden layers |

ReLU became popular after Nair and Hinton reported that rectified units trained well in 2010,<Cite id="nair2010" /> and the CS231n notes recommend it as a default for hidden layers.<Cite id="cs231n-nn1" /> In the example above, the sigmoid would have output $1/(1+e^{1}) \approx 0.269$ instead of $0$.

<Callout type="pitfall">
"The bias is optional decoration." It is not: without it, the neuron's boundary between "on" and "off" must pass through the origin, which rules out many simple patterns.
</Callout>

## From one neuron to the perceptron

In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt described the **perceptron**, a machine built around one neuron with a step activation that learned its weights from examples.<Cite id="rosenblatt1958" /> Its learning rule changes the weights only when the prediction is wrong, and it is guaranteed to find a separating line when one exists.<Cite id="wiki-perceptron" />

With weights $w = [1, 1]$ and bias $b = -1.5$, a step neuron computes logical AND: only the input $(1, 1)$ gives $z = 0.5 > 0$. No single line, however, separates the XOR pattern, where $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ are "on" and $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are "off". Minsky and Papert's 1969 analysis of this limit cooled interest in perceptrons for years; adding a hidden layer removes it.<Cite id="wiki-perceptron" /><Cite id="d2l-mlp" />

## Many neurons side by side

A **layer** is a group of neurons that all read the same inputs, each with its own weights and bias. A layer with $n_\text{in}$ inputs and $n_\text{out}$ neurons therefore has $n_\text{in} \times n_\text{out}$ weights plus $n_\text{out}$ biases.<Cite id="d2l-mlp" /> For example, a layer that reads a 28 × 28 pixel image (784 inputs) with 128 neurons has $784 \times 128 + 128 = 100{,}480$ parameters, which is why even small networks are trained automatically rather than tuned by hand.<Cite id="cs231n-nn1" />

Writing all the weights of a layer as a grid of numbers (a matrix) lets libraries compute every neuron's weighted sum in one operation. Later lessons use exactly this trick; the single-neuron formula above is what each row of that computation does.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch6" />

## Summary

- A neuron computes a weighted sum $z = \sum_i w_i x_i + b$ and then an activation $a = \phi(z)$.
- Weights scale the inputs, the bias shifts the switching point, and the activation adds non-linearity.
- ReLU is the common default in hidden layers; the sigmoid maps to a probability-like range.
- A single neuron can only draw a straight boundary, so it cannot learn XOR; layers fix this.

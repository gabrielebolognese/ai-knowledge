**Artificial intelligence** (AI) is the field of computer science that builds systems able to perform tasks that would call for intelligence if a person did them, such as recognising images, understanding language or playing games.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" /> **Machine learning** is the part of AI in which a program learns its behaviour from examples instead of following rules written by hand, and **deep learning** is the part of machine learning that uses <Term slug="neural-network">neural networks</Term> with many layers.<Cite id="d2l-intro" /> This course teaches deep learning from the ground up, so it helps to know where it sits.

## Three nested fields

The three terms are often used as if they meant the same thing, but each one sits inside the previous one.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" />

<Figure src="/figures/beginner/ai-ml-dl.svg" alt="Three nested ellipses: artificial intelligence on the outside, machine learning inside it, deep learning innermost." caption="Deep learning is a kind of machine learning, which is a kind of AI." credit="Own work, after Goodfellow et al. (2016), figure 1.4" license="CC BY 4.0" licenseUrl="https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/" />

- **AI** is the widest circle. A chess program that searches millions of positions with rules written by people is AI, even though it learns nothing.<Cite id="wiki-ai-history" />
- **Machine learning** covers any method that improves at a task through experience. A textbook definition says a program learns if its performance on a task, measured in some way, gets better with more experience (data).<Cite id="goodfellow2016-ml" /> Fitting a straight line to house prices is already machine learning.
- **Deep learning** uses networks of many simple units, the <Term slug="artificial-neuron">artificial neurons</Term>, stacked in layers. Each layer turns its input into a slightly more abstract description, for example from pixels to edges, from edges to strokes and from strokes to "this is a 7".<Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" />

<Analogy breaks="Real dolls are only containers; the fields overlap in methods and people, and the borders between them are not sharp.">
The fields are like Russian nesting dolls. AI is the biggest doll, machine learning fits inside it, and deep learning fits inside machine learning.
</Analogy>

## Rules versus learning

In classic programming a person writes the rules and the computer applies them to data. In machine learning a person supplies data together with the desired answers, and an algorithm finds rules that reproduce those answers.<Cite id="d2l-intro" /> The rules it finds are not readable sentences: they are thousands or millions of numbers called <Term slug="parameter">parameters</Term>, which the next lesson explains.

<Analogy breaks="A cook can read and explain a recipe; a trained network's 'recipe' is a pile of numbers that is hard to interpret.">
Classic programming is handing the computer a recipe. Machine learning is handing it hundreds of finished dishes with their ingredient lists and letting it work out a recipe that produces them.
</Analogy>

The term "machine learning" was popularised by Arthur Samuel, whose 1959 checkers program improved by playing games against itself.<Cite id="samuel1959" />

## Three ways a machine learns

Machine learning is usually divided into three settings, depending on what kind of feedback the program gets.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-ml" />

| Setting | What the program receives | Example in this course |
|---|---|---|
| <Term slug="supervised-learning">Supervised</Term> | Inputs paired with correct answers (labels) | Images of digits tagged 0-9 |
| <Term slug="unsupervised-learning">Unsupervised</Term> (and self-supervised) | Inputs only; it finds structure itself | Predicting the next character of a text |
| <Term slug="reinforcement-learning">Reinforcement</Term> | Rewards or penalties for its actions | A game agent rewarded for winning |

Most of this course is supervised learning. The small text and code models use a self-supervised trick: the "label" for each position in a text is simply the next character, so no person has to tag anything.<Cite id="d2l-intro" />

## A short timeline

The ideas behind deep learning are much older than the recent headlines.<Cite id="wiki-ai-history" />

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1943 | Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts describe a mathematical model of a neuron as a simple logic unit.<Cite id="mcculloch1943" /> |
| 1950 | Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the imitation game, later called the Turing test.<Cite id="turing1950" /> |
| 1956 | The Dartmouth summer workshop, proposed in 1955, gives the field the name "artificial intelligence".<Cite id="wiki-dartmouth" /> |
| 1958 | Frank Rosenblatt's <Term slug="perceptron" /> is a neuron that learns its weights from examples.<Cite id="rosenblatt1958" /> |
| 1970s and late 1980s | Funding collapses twice after promises are not met, periods now called "AI winters".<Cite id="wiki-ai-winter" /> |
| 1986 | Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams show that backpropagation can train networks with hidden layers.<Cite id="rumelhart1986" /> |
| 2012 | AlexNet, a deep network trained on GPUs, wins the ImageNet image-recognition contest with a top-5 error of 15.3%, against 26.2% for the runner-up.<Cite id="krizhevsky2012" /> |
| 2017 | The Transformer architecture appears; it later powers large language models.<Cite id="vaswani2017" /> |
| 2018 | Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun receive the ACM Turing Award for their work on deep neural networks.<Cite id="acm-turing-2018" /> |
| 2024 | John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton receive the Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational work that enables machine learning with artificial neural networks.<Cite id="nobel-2024" /> |

The 2012 jump did not come from a brand-new algorithm. It came from three things arriving together: large labelled datasets, fast graphics processors (GPUs) and better training tricks such as the <Term slug="relu" /> activation and <Term slug="dropout" />.<Cite id="krizhevsky2012" /><Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" />

## What neural networks do, and what they do not

A neural network is a large mathematical function with adjustable numbers. Training adjusts those numbers until the function's outputs match the examples well. The result can recognise a digit or suggest the next word of code, but it does so by applying learned patterns, not by understanding the way a person does.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" />

<Callout type="pitfall">
"Neural networks work like the brain." They were loosely inspired by brain cells, but an artificial neuron is only a weighted sum followed by a simple function. Modern deep learning is guided by mathematics and engineering much more than by neuroscience.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-intro" />
</Callout>

Two more points worth keeping in mind:

- **AI is not only deep learning.** Search-based game engines, route planners and rule-based systems are AI without neural networks.<Cite id="wiki-ai-history" />
- **Deep learning was not invented in 2012.** The neuron, the perceptron and backpropagation are decades old; data and computing power caught up later.<Cite id="d2l-intro" />

## Summary

- **AI ⊃ machine learning ⊃ deep learning**: each field sits inside the one before it.
- Machine learning finds its rules from examples; deep learning does this with many-layered neural networks.
- The three learning settings are supervised (labelled answers), unsupervised or self-supervised (no labels) and reinforcement (rewards).
- Key milestones: the neuron model (1943), the perceptron (1958), backpropagation (1986), AlexNet (2012) and the Transformer (2017).
- Neural networks learn patterns from data; they are inspired by, but not models of, the brain.

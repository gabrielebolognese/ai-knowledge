**Gradient descent** is a method for finding the lowest point of a function by repeatedly taking small steps downhill, where "downhill" is worked out from the function's slope.<Cite id="wiki-gd" /> In machine learning the function is the <Term slug="loss-function">loss</Term>, which measures how wrong a model is, and the steps change the model's parameters, such as the weights of each <Term slug="artificial-neuron">neuron</Term>.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch4" />

## The idea

A model's loss depends on its parameters. If you could see the loss as a landscape, good parameters would sit at the bottom of a valley. The <Term slug="gradient" /> of the loss is a list of slopes, one per parameter, and it points in the direction where the loss rises fastest.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch4" /> Taking a small step in the **opposite** direction lowers the loss, at least for small enough steps.<Cite id="cs231n-opt" />

The method is old: it is usually credited to Augustin-Louis Cauchy, who suggested it in 1847.<Cite id="wiki-gd" /> Its noisy cousin, which uses a random sample of the data at each step, traces back to Robbins and Monro's 1951 work on stochastic approximation.<Cite id="robbins1951" />

<Analogy breaks="A hiker can look around; gradient descent only knows the slope at the exact spot where it stands.">
Imagine walking down a hill in thick fog with a fixed stride. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under your feet, so you step that way and repeat.
</Analogy>

## The update rule, piece by piece

Take the simplest possible loss, $f(w) = (w-3)^2$. Its minimum is at $w = 3$, and its <Term slug="derivative" /> is $2(w-3)$. Start at $w_0 = 0$ with a <Term slug="learning-rate" /> of $\eta = 0.1$.

  <Step tex="w_2 = 0.6 - 0.1 \cdot \hl{2(0.6 - 3)} = 0.6 + 0.48 = 1.08" words="Repeat from the new point. The slope is gentler now, so the step is smaller." />
  <Step tex="w_3 = 1.08 - 0.1 \cdot 2(1.08 - 3) = \hl{1.464}" words="Each step shrinks the distance to 3 by the same factor, 1 − 2η = 0.8." example="distance 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536" />

The last line is the update used to train every network in this course; libraries such as [PyTorch's `torch.optim`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/optim.html) package it, together with faster variants, as "optimisers".<Cite id="pytorch-optim" />

## Why following the slope works

Near any point, a smooth function looks almost like a straight line, and the derivative is that line's slope. Moving a small distance $\epsilon$ against the slope changes the function by roughly $-\epsilon$ times the squared slope, which is negative whenever the slope is not zero, so a small enough step always goes downhill.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch4" /> The catch is the word *small*: the straight-line picture is only accurate close to the current point, which is exactly why the step size matters.<Cite id="d2l-gd" />

When the gradient is zero the method stops moving. That happens at a minimum, but also at a maximum or at a **saddle point**, where the surface curves up in one direction and down in another.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch4" /> For the bowl-shaped $f(w) = (w-3)^2$ there is only one such point, the minimum, so gradient descent cannot get lost. Neural-network losses are far bumpier, and gradient descent then finds a good low point rather than a guaranteed lowest one; in practice those low points usually work well.<Cite id="cs231n-opt" />

## Choosing the learning rate

The learning rate $\eta$ is the step size, and it is the setting that most often decides whether training works.<Cite id="mlcc-hparams" /> In the example, the distance to the minimum is multiplied by $1 - 2\eta$ at each step:<Cite id="d2l-gd" />

| Learning rate | Factor $1 - 2\eta$ | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.8 | Steady progress towards 3 |
| 0.5 | 0 | Lands on 3 in one step (lucky for this function) |
| 1.1 | −1.2 | Overshoots further each time: training diverges |

A rate that is too small wastes time; one that is too large makes the loss bounce or explode.<Cite id="mlcc-hparams" /> There is rarely a single "perfect" value known in advance, so practitioners search for it on a logarithmic scale, trying values such as 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 rather than 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3.<Cite id="cs231n-nn3" />

<Callout type="tip">
If the loss becomes `nan` within the first few steps, lower the learning rate by a factor of 10 before changing anything else.
</Callout>

## From one number to a whole network

A neural network has thousands or millions of parameters, but the rule is the same: compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight and bias, then move each one a little against its own slope.<Cite id="goodfellow-ch4" /> Computing all those slopes efficiently is the job of backpropagation, covered later in the course.

Most real training uses **mini-batch stochastic gradient descent**: the gradient is estimated from a small random batch of examples rather than the whole data set, which is far cheaper per step.<Cite id="cs231n-opt" /><Cite id="robbins1951" /> Each estimate is a little noisy, so the path wobbles on its way down, but thousands of cheap, slightly noisy steps usually beat a few exact and expensive ones. The number of examples per step is the **batch size**, and one full pass through the training data is called an **epoch**; both are settings you choose, alongside the learning rate.<Cite id="mlcc-hparams" />

## Summary

- Gradient descent lowers a loss by stepping parameters against the gradient: $w \leftarrow w - \eta \nabla_w L$.
- The gradient points uphill, which is why the update subtracts it.
- The learning rate sets the step size: too small is slow, too large diverges.
- Neural networks use the same rule on every parameter, usually with mini-batches.

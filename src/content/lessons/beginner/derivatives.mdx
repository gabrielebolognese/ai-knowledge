The **derivative** of a function measures how quickly its output changes when its input is nudged a tiny amount; on a graph it is the slope of the curve at one point.<Cite id="wiki-derivative" /> Deep learning depends on derivatives because training a network means asking, for every <Term slug="weight" />, "if I nudge this number, does the error go up or down, and how fast?"<Cite id="goodfellow2016-numerical" /> This lesson builds the idea from school-level slopes and needs no calculus background.

## Slope as rise over run

For a straight line, the **slope** is how far the line goes up (the *rise*) for each step to the right (the *run*).<Cite id="wiki-slope" /> Take the line $y = 2x + 1$:

- at $x = 1$ it is at $y = 3$;
- at $x = 3$ it is at $y = 7$;
- rise $= 7 - 3 = 4$, run $= 3 - 1 = 2$, so the slope is $4 / 2 = 2$.

Any two points on this line give the same answer, 2, because a straight line is equally steep everywhere. A positive slope means "going up", a negative slope means "going down" and a slope of 0 means flat.<Cite id="khan-calc1" />

## Curves change steepness

A curve such as $y = x^2$ is gentle near $x = 0$ and steep further out, so "the slope" depends on where you stand.<Cite id="d2l-calculus" /> The trick is to measure rise over run between two points that are very close together. As the second point slides towards the first, the ratio settles on one number: the derivative at that point, written $f'(x)$ ("f prime of x").<Cite id="wiki-derivative" />

<Analogy breaks="A car's speed changes smoothly over time; the functions in a network can have sharp corners (like ReLU at 0) where the slope jumps.">
A derivative is like a car's speedometer. The trip so far is the function, and the speedometer shows how fast that distance is changing right now, not the average over the whole trip.
</Analogy>

The idea goes back to Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who developed calculus independently in the late 17th century.<Cite id="wiki-derivative" /> The 3Blue1Brown series [Essence of calculus](https://www.3blue1brown.com/topics/calculus) animates the same "zoom in until the curve looks straight" picture.<Cite id="3b1b-calculus" />

The ratio with a small but non-zero $h$ is called a <Term slug="finite-difference" />. It only *estimates* the derivative, but it is so easy to compute that programmers use it to check their exact formulas, a practice known as gradient checking.<Cite id="cs231n-optim1" />

## Estimating a slope in Python

The finite-difference recipe fits in a few lines of plain Python:

```python
def f(x):
    return x ** 2

def slope(f, x, h):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h

for h in [1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    print(f"h = {h:<6} slope ≈ {slope(f, 3, h):.4f}")
```

It prints `7.0000`, `6.1000`, `6.0100` and `6.0010`, the same numbers as the steps above. The error shrinks along with $h$.<Cite id="d2l-calculus" />

<Callout type="pitfall">
"Smaller h is always better." Not on a computer. Numbers are stored with limited precision, so an extremely small $h$ (such as $10^{-15}$) makes the subtraction lose almost all its digits. Values around $10^{-5}$ are a common compromise.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-numerical" />
</Callout>

## A few rules instead of limits

Working out limits every time would be slow, so mathematicians collected rules that give the derivative directly.<Cite id="khan-calc1" /> These cover almost everything in this course:

| Rule | Function | Derivative | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constant | $c$ | $0$ | $7 \to 0$ |
| Power | $x^n$ | $n x^{n-1}$ | $x^3 \to 3x^2$ |
| Constant factor | $c \cdot f(x)$ | $c \cdot f'(x)$ | $5x^2 \to 10x$ |
| Sum | $f(x) + g(x)$ | $f'(x) + g'(x)$ | $x^2 + x \to 2x + 1$ |
| <Term slug="exponential-function">Exponential</Term> | $e^x$ | $e^x$ | its own slope |
| <Term slug="natural-logarithm">Natural log</Term> | $\ln x$ | $1/x$ | at $x = 2$: $0.5$ |

The number $e \approx 2.718$ is a mathematical constant like $\pi$. The function $e^x$ has the unusual property of being its own derivative, which is one reason it appears in the <Term slug="softmax" /> and <Term slug="sigmoid" /> functions later in the track.<Cite id="d2l-calculus" />

## Why a loss curve's slope matters

In machine learning the function we care about is the <Term slug="loss-function" />: a number that measures how wrong a model is, which depends on the model's parameters.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-numerical" /> Take a model with one weight $w$ and the loss $L(w) = (w - 3)^2$, which is smallest at $w = 3$.

The sign tells you which way to move and the size tells you how steep the ground is. <Term slug="gradient-descent">Gradient descent</Term> turns this into a rule: move the weight a small step against the slope, and repeat.<Cite id="cs231n-optim1" />

## Where slopes need care

Two situations come up often in deep learning, and both are about the slope rather than the function's value.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-numerical" />

- **Corners.** The <Term slug="relu">ReLU</Term> activation, $\max(0, x)$, has slope 0 to the left of 0 and slope 1 to the right. Exactly at 0 there is a sharp corner and no single slope. Libraries simply pick 0 (or 1) there, and training works fine in practice because inputs land exactly on 0 very rarely.<Cite id="d2l-calculus" />
- **Flat stretches.** Where a function is almost flat, its slope is almost 0, so the slope gives training very little information about which way to go. The <Term slug="sigmoid" /> curve flattens out for large positive or negative inputs, which is one reason later lessons prefer ReLU in hidden layers.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-numerical" />

Neither case changes the basic idea: the derivative is still "rise over run for a tiny run", wherever it exists.

## Summary

- A derivative $f'(x)$ is the slope of $f$ at one point: how much the output changes per tiny nudge of the input.
- It can be estimated with a finite difference $\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$ for a small $h$.
- The constant, power, constant-factor and sum rules give exact derivatives quickly; $e^x$ is its own derivative and $\ln x$ has derivative $1/x$.
- The slope of a loss tells training which way to move a weight: positive slope, decrease it; negative slope, increase it; zero, stop.

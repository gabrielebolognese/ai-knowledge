**Overfitting** happens when a model fits its training data so closely, noise and quirks included, that it does worse on new data; **regularization** is the family of techniques that push a model towards solutions that generalize.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-reg" /> Modern networks have enough <Term slug="parameter">parameters</Term> to memorize entire training sets, so recognising overfitting and controlling it is part of every real training run.<Cite id="zhang2017" />

## Underfitting and overfitting

Training is judged on two curves: the loss on the training data and the loss on a held-out <Term slug="validation-set">validation set</Term>.<Cite id="cs231n-nn2" />

| What the curves show | Diagnosis | First things to try |
|---|---|---|
| Both losses high and close together | **Underfitting**: too simple or too little training | Bigger model, train longer, higher learning rate |
| Training loss low, validation loss stalls then rises | **Overfitting**: memorizing the training set | More data, augmentation, weight decay, dropout, early stopping |
| Both low and close | Good fit | Stop, then measure on the test set once |

The classic illustration fits polynomials to 15 noisy points of a sine curve. A straight line (degree 1) misses the shape, with training and validation errors of 0.316 and 0.351. Degree 3 follows the curve (training error 0.019). Degree 12 passes almost exactly through every training point (error 0.000) but swings wildly between them, and its validation error explodes to about 10.<Cite id="d2l-weight-decay" />

<Analogy breaks="A student can be told to stop memorizing; a network has to be steered there by its training setup, data and regularization.">
Overfitting is like memorizing last year's exam answers: perfect on that paper, lost on a new one. The validation set is the mock exam you check yourself on; the test set is the real exam, sat once.
</Analogy>

## The bias-variance trade-off, and its caveats

The traditional explanation splits the error on new data into **bias** (systematic error from a model that is too simple) and **variance** (sensitivity to the particular training sample, typical of very flexible models). Increasing capacity lowers bias and raises variance, so test error traces a U-shape with a sweet spot in the middle.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-reg" />

Deep learning complicates this picture. Zhang et al. showed that standard image networks can reach zero training error on CIFAR-10 even when every label is replaced by a random one, yet the same networks generalize well on the real labels; capacity alone does not predict generalization.<Cite id="zhang2017" /> Belkin et al. described **double descent**: as models grow past the point where they can fit the training data exactly, test error can fall again.<Cite id="belkin2019" /> The practical lesson stays the same: judge models by validation performance, not by parameter counts or training loss.

## Weight decay (L2 regularization)

<Term slug="weight-decay" /> adds a penalty on large weights to the loss. The penalty is $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2$, half the sum of all squared weights times a strength $\lambda$, so the network only keeps a weight large if the data really needs it.<Cite id="d2l-weight-decay" />

    { symbol: '\\eta', meaning: 'learning rate' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="L_\text{total} = L + \hl{\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\textstyle\sum_i w_i^2}" words="Add the penalty to the loss. The one-half is there to cancel a 2 in the next step." />
  <Step tex="\frac{\partial L_\text{total}}{\partial w} = g + \hl{\lambda w}" words="Differentiate: the penalty contributes λw to each weight's gradient." />
  <Step tex="w \leftarrow w - \eta(g + \lambda w) = \hl{(1 - \eta\lambda)}\,w - \eta g" words="Plug into gradient descent. Before the usual step, every weight is multiplied by a number slightly below 1: it decays." />
  <Step tex="w = 2,\ \lambda = 0.01,\ \eta = 0.1,\ g = 0:\quad w \leftarrow (1 - 0.001) \cdot 2 = \hl{1.998}" words="With numbers: a weight the data does not push on shrinks a little every step." example="2 → 1.998 → 1.996 …" />
</MathSteps>

In PyTorch the strength is the optimizer's `weight_decay` argument. For SGD the two formulations above are identical, but for adaptive optimizers such as Adam they are not: Loshchilov and Hutter showed that applying the decay directly to the weights, as `torch.optim.AdamW` does, works better than putting an L2 term into Adam's gradient.<Cite id="loshchilov2017" />

<Analogy breaks="A released rubber band snaps straight back; a weight only shrinks by the small factor (1 − ηλ) per step, and the band's strength λ is a setting you choose.">
Weight decay is a rubber band tied from every weight back to zero. The data has to keep pulling to hold a weight far out; weights it does not need drift back.
</Analogy>

## Dropout

<Term slug="dropout" /> randomly sets each unit's output to zero with probability $p$ during training, a different random pattern for every batch. No unit can rely on one specific partner, and the network behaves somewhat like an average over many thinned sub-networks.<Cite id="srivastava2014" /> Srivastava et al. reported clear improvements from dropout on vision, speech and text benchmarks, typically with $p = 0.5$ for hidden units.<Cite id="srivastava2014" />

PyTorch implements **inverted dropout**: the surviving values are scaled up during training so that nothing needs to change at test time.<Cite id="pt-dropout" />

<MathSteps
  title="Inverted dropout with p = 0.5"
  legend={[
    { symbol: 'a', meaning: 'activations entering the layer' },
    { symbol: 'm', meaning: 'random keep mask (1 = keep)' },
    { symbol: 'p', meaning: 'drop probability' },
  ]}
>
  <Step tex="a = [1, 2, 3, 4],\quad m = [1, 0, 1, 0]" words="Draw a random mask: each unit is kept with probability 1 − p = 0.5." />
  <Step tex="a \odot m = \hl{[1, 0, 3, 0]}" words="Multiply element by element: dropped units output 0." />
  <Step tex="\frac{a \odot m}{1 - p} = \hl{[2, 0, 6, 0]}" words="Scale the survivors by 1/(1 − p) = 2." />
  <Step tex="\mathbb{E}[\text{output}_i] = (1-p)\cdot\frac{a_i}{1-p} + p \cdot 0 = \hl{a_i}" words="On average each unit outputs its original value, so in evaluation mode the layer simply passes the input through." />
</MathSteps>

That is why `model.eval()` matters: it turns dropout into the identity. Leaving the model in training mode during validation gives noisy, pessimistic scores.<Cite id="d2l-dropout" />

## Early stopping and data augmentation

<Term slug="early-stopping" /> watches the validation loss and keeps the checkpoint from the best epoch, stopping once there has been no improvement for a few epochs (the *patience*). It costs nothing and is among the most widely used regularizers.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-reg" /> Use a patience of several epochs, because validation curves are noisy and a single bad epoch is not a trend.

<Term slug="data-augmentation" /> creates new training examples by transforming existing ones in ways that keep the label, for example small shifts, rotations or crops of an image. More, and more varied, data is the most reliable fix for overfitting; augmentation is the cheapest way to get it.<Cite id="goodfellow2016-reg" /> The transforms must make sense for the task: flipping a "6" upside down gives a "9". The doodle project applies this in [Data augmentation and regularization for sketches](/course/project-doodles/doodle-augmentation/).

<Callout type="tip">
Regularize one thing at a time, starting from a model that can overfit. If the model cannot even fit the training data, regularization only makes things worse; fix underfitting first, as [Debugging neural network training](/course/intermediate/debugging-training/) explains.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Overfitting means low training loss with a validation loss that stalls or rises; underfitting means both stay high.
- Weight decay adds $\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2$ to the loss, which multiplies every weight by $(1 - \eta\lambda)$ each step; use `AdamW` with Adam.
- Dropout zeroes random units during training and scales the rest by $1/(1-p)$; `model.eval()` switches it off.
- Early stopping keeps the best validation checkpoint; augmentation and more data are the strongest fixes.
- Large networks can memorize even random labels, so trust validation performance, not training loss.

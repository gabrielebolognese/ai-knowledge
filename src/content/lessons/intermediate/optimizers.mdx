An **optimizer** is the rule that turns gradients into weight updates. Plain <Term slug="gradient-descent" /> is the simplest optimizer; the variants in this lesson add memory of past gradients (momentum) and per-parameter step sizes (RMSProp and Adam), which usually train neural networks faster and more reliably.<Cite id="d2l-optimization" /> In PyTorch every one of them is a class in [`torch.optim`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/optim.html) with the same `zero_grad()` and `step()` interface.<Cite id="pt-optim" />

## How much data per step

Gradient descent can estimate the gradient from different amounts of data.<Cite id="d2l-optimization" />

| Variant | Examples per step | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Full-batch | The whole training set | Exact gradient, but one step is very expensive |
| Stochastic (SGD) | 1 | Very cheap and very noisy |
| Mini-batch | Typically 32 to 512 | The standard compromise, and fast on vector hardware |

The stochastic idea goes back to Robbins and Monro's 1951 work on stochastic approximation.<Cite id="robbins1951" /> In current practice "SGD" almost always means mini-batch SGD. The noise from small batches is not purely a cost: it lets the optimizer jiggle out of flat or poor regions.<Cite id="cs231n-nn3" /> One pass over the training set is an <Term slug="epoch" />, and one mini-batch update is an *iteration*.

## Momentum

Plain gradient descent forgets everything at each step. In a long, narrow valley the gradient points mostly across the valley, so the path zig-zags from wall to wall and moves slowly along the floor. **Momentum** keeps a running velocity $v$ that adds up recent gradients. Components that keep the same sign build up, and components that flip sign cancel out.<Cite id="d2l-optimization" /><Cite id="sutskever2013" />

<Analogy breaks="A real ball has mass and friction set by physics; here β is a number you choose, and the 'ball' can pass through hills that a real one could not.">
Momentum works like a heavy ball rolling downhill. It gathers speed on a steady slope and keeps rolling through small bumps, instead of stopping to re-check the direction at every step.
</Analogy>

Sutskever and colleagues showed that momentum, together with good initialization, was crucial for training deep and recurrent networks with SGD.<Cite id="sutskever2013" /> The same velocity that speeds things up can also overshoot: in the script below, momentum passes $w = 3$ on its third step and has to roll back.

## Per-parameter step sizes: RMSProp

Different weights can see gradients of very different sizes. **RMSProp**, proposed by Geoffrey Hinton in a 2012 Coursera lecture rather than in a paper, keeps a running average $s$ of each parameter's *squared* gradient and divides the step by $\sqrt{s}$.<Cite id="hinton-rmsprop" /> Parameters with consistently large gradients get smaller steps, and parameters with tiny gradients get larger ones.<Cite id="d2l-optimization" />

## Adam

**Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014) combines the two ideas. It keeps a momentum-like average of gradients $m$ (the *first moment*) and an RMSProp-like average of squared gradients $v$ (the *second moment*). Both start at zero, so in the first steps they are biased towards zero; Adam divides them by $1 - \beta^t$ to correct this.<Cite id="kingma2014" /> The paper's defaults are $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$ and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.<Cite id="kingma2014" /><Cite id="d2l-adam" />

  <Step tex="w \leftarrow w - \eta\, \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v} + \epsilon}" words="The general Adam update, applied to every parameter separately." />

Two practical consequences follow from this. Adam still needs a learning rate: $10^{-3}$ is a common start for small networks and about $3 \times 10^{-4}$ for Transformers.<Cite id="cs231n-nn3" /> And on this simple bowl Adam is slower than plain gradient descent, because its steps stay near $\eta = 0.1$ until it gets close. Its advantage shows on real losses, where gradient scales differ wildly between parameters.<Cite id="d2l-adam" />

### AdamW

Adding `weight_decay` to `torch.optim.Adam` puts the decay term into the gradient, where it is then divided by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, so weights with large gradients are barely regularised. Loshchilov and Hutter proposed applying the decay directly to the weights instead ("decoupled weight decay"). This is `torch.optim.AdamW`, the standard optimizer for Transformers.<Cite id="loshchilov2017" /><Cite id="pt-optim" />

## Choosing an optimizer and a learning rate

A reasonable default order is:<Cite id="cs231n-nn3" /><Cite id="d2l-optimization" />

1. **Adam or AdamW** with $\eta = 10^{-3}$ for a first working model: robust and forgiving.
2. **SGD with momentum 0.9** when you have time to tune it. On image tasks a well-tuned SGD often generalises slightly better than Adam, but results vary.
3. Change the learning rate by factors of about 3 to 10, never in small linear steps. The lesson on [tuning hyperparameters](/course/intermediate/hyperparameter-tuning/) shows how to search for it.

<Callout type="pitfall">
"Adam doesn't need a learning rate." It does. Ten times too high still diverges, and ten times too low still crawls.
</Callout>

### Learning-rate schedules

A <Term slug="learning-rate" /> that suits the start of training is usually too large at the end, so most training runs change it over time. Common schedules are step decay, cosine decay to zero, and a short **warmup** that starts small and ramps up during the first few hundred steps, which helps Adam on Transformers.<Cite id="d2l-optimization" /> Smith's cyclical schedules, which move the rate up and down between bounds, are another option.<Cite id="smith2015" /> In PyTorch a scheduler wraps the optimizer and is stepped *after* `optimizer.step()`:<Cite id="pt-optim" />

```python
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.01)
warmup = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LinearLR(opt, start_factor=0.1, total_iters=100)
cosine = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=900)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.SequentialLR(opt, schedulers=[warmup, cosine], milestones=[100])

for step in range(1000):
    ...                  # forward, loss, opt.zero_grad(), loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    sched.step()         # once per batch here; many vision recipes step once per epoch
```

## Summary

- Mini-batch SGD estimates the gradient from a small random batch: cheap, slightly noisy and the standard choice.
- Momentum adds up past gradients ($v \leftarrow \beta v + g$), speeding up steady directions and damping zig-zags.
- RMSProp and Adam give each parameter its own step size; Adam's bias correction makes early steps about $\eta$ in size.
- Use AdamW for Transformers, start Adam near $10^{-3}$, and tune the learning rate on a log scale with a schedule.
